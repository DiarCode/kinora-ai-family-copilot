## Decision
| Band | Decision | Main reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Expecting → 6** | **Entry point / MVP focus** | Steepest couple-conflict dip; strongest early-development evidence; the buyer is ~90% inside 25–45 |
| **6–12** | **Core** (Class 4 · Open Road) | Values, habits, school, siblings. Parent influence is strong; the buyer is mostly inside 25–45 |
| **10–12** | **Bridge** inside Class 4: first phone, social media, puberty | Half of kids have a phone by 11; ages 10–14 are "pivotal" (Dahl 2018) |
| **13–15** | **Roadmap: "Class 5 · Road Test"**, a different mechanic (coach, not director) | Teens still learn from parents, but the skills differ, and only ~42–70% of their parents are 25–45 |
| **16–18** | **Out of scope** | Most of their parents are over 45 (outside the case rule); spending shifts to college admissions and clinical care |

## 1. Testing our assumptions
| Assumption | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| "After ~12, children stop listening to parents" | ❌ **Not supported.** The *role* changes; influence stays | **Family connectedness protects teens against nearly every risk behavior** (Add Health, n=12,118, Resnick et al. 1997, *JAMA*) [S]. **What parents know about a teen comes mostly from the teen's own disclosure, and disclosure predicts less delinquency** (Stattin & Kerr 2000) [S]. Values like religion and politics keep transmitting into adulthood, with a warm relationship the key (Bengtson; Jennings, Stoker & Bowers 2009) [S/SEC] |
| "Teens are more independent; peers take over" | ✅ Partly true | Peers raise teen risk-taking much more than adults' (Gardner & Steinberg 2005) [S]. Teens ask parents for advice less as they age: 58% of younger teens vs 33% of older teens (Pew 2013) [S/SEC] |
| "Adolescence doesn't matter as much as early childhood" | ❌ Wrong | Adolescence is a second high-stakes window; **ages 10–14 are pivotal** (Dahl et al. 2018, *Nature*) [S]. Caveat: hard proof that it's a true neural "sensitive period" is still limited (Fuhrmann et al. 2015) [S] |
| "Earlier is always better for parenting programs" | ⚠️ Not exactly | For ages 2–11, **no evidence that earlier is better**: programs work across the range (Gardner et al. 2019, 169 trials) [S]. Behavioral parent training works best for younger kids; for teens, cognitive-behavioral and family approaches work better (McCart et al. 2006) [S] |
| "Teen programs don't work" | ❌ Wrong, but engagement is hard | The Strengthening Families Program 10–14 shows effects into the 20s [S]. The Family Check-Up reduces substance use, but **only ~23% of parents engaged** [S], so a different, lighter mechanic is needed |
| "US kids move out early (unlike Europe, Russia, KZ)" | ⚠️ **Only partly true now** | **57% of US 18–24-year-olds live with their parents** (Census CPS 2024) [SEC], and 44% of 18–34s got money from their parents last year (Pew 2024) [S]. The EU average leaving age is 26.2: earlier in the Nordics (~21–22), later in southern Europe (~30) (Eurostat) [S]. The US sits between. Only ~25% of 16-year-olds have a driver's license (vs ~46% in 1983) [SEC]. No good Kazakhstan figure was found |
| "Teens can just leave home at 16" | ❌ Rare | The legal age is 18; emancipation is rare. Estrangement exists (27% of adults estranged from some relative; a minority involve a parent and child, Pillemer) but mostly happens in adulthood |

**Conclusion:** your instinct was right that **teens matter and parents still shape them**. But the parent's job flips from *directing* to *coaching*: protecting the relationship, earning disclosure, de-escalating conflict, and handling digital life and mental health. Conflict *intensity* peaks in early-to-mid adolescence (Laursen et al. 1998) [S]. That's a different product mechanic, so it should be a separate class later, not mixed into the MVP.

## 2. The business argument: the 25–45 buyer rule
Mean age at first birth is **27.6** (NCHS 2024) [S]. Estimated share of parents who are themselves **aged 25–45**, by child age [E, ±10 pts]:

| Child age | Mothers aged 25–45 | Fathers aged 25–45 |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | ~88–90% | ~88% |
| 6–12 | ~85–88% | ~70–75% |
| 13–15 | ~60–70% | ~42–50% |
| 16–17 | ~40–55% | ~30–35% |

→ **0–12 fits the case's buyer rule. 13–15 is borderline. 16–17 mostly falls outside it.** That's a concrete, rule-based reason the jury will accept.

**Market:** parents of teens do spend, but mostly on **monitoring** (Life360 ~$372M revenue in 2024, 2.3M paying circles; Bark $14/month) and **clinical care and college prep**, not on parent skills training [S/SEC]. Pricing evidence for teen *skills* products is thin. Meanwhile the proven subscription (Good Inside, $34M) is centered on kids aged 2–10 [SEC]. **89% of parents worry about teen mental health** (Pew 2025) [S], so the need is real. Treat it as an expansion opportunity to validate.

## 3. Updated license classes
| Class | Child age | Parent role | Signature skills |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learner's Permit | Expecting | Prepare as a team | Night-shift plan, fight-fair pact |
| Class 1 · Night Shift | 0–1 | Caregiver team | Sleep, load-sharing, handoffs |
| Class 2 · Calm Driver | 1–3 | Calm limit-setter | Tantrums, one-voice limits, repair in front of the kid |
| Class 3 · School Zone | 3–6 | Routine builder | Routines, emotion coaching, pre-K and kindergarten |
| Class 4 · Open Road | 6–12 (bridge 10–12) | Guide | Values, siblings, school, **first phone and social media contract**, puberty talks |
| **Class 5 · Road Test** *(roadmap)* | **13–15** | **Coach / consultant** | Earning disclosure (not surveillance), conflict de-escalation, autonomy negotiation, mental-health check-ins, digital life |
| (16–18) | — | — | Out of scope (buyer mostly over 45) |

## 4. How to say it in the pitch (if asked "why 0–12?")
> "We start where the evidence and the buyer overlap: the first twelve years, when ~85–90% of parents are 25–45 and the research on parent influence is strongest. Influence doesn't stop at 12. Teens whose families stay connected are protected from almost every risk (Resnick 1997, *JAMA*), and teens' own disclosure, not surveillance, is what keeps parents informed (Stattin & Kerr 2000). But the parent's role changes from director to coach. So 13–15 is our next class, 'Road Test', with its own mechanic."

**Best citations:**
1. **Gardner 2019:** programs work across ages 2–11.
2. **Resnick 1997:** family connectedness protects teens.
3. **Stattin & Kerr 2000:** disclosure over surveillance.
4. **Dahl 2018:** ages 10–14 are pivotal. Use with the Fuhrmann 2015 caveat.
